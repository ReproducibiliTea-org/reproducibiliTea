**Making research data openly available enables reuse of the data to check the reproducibility of the findings, test new statistical models, or for other uses, such as in meta-analyses. Sharing data publicly is done with the expectation that the data will be reused in a responsible manner. This is not always the case, however, as became clear to me recently. I noticed that one of my openly available datasets had been reused in three papers. These papers involved the testing of machine learning models for EEG-based deception detection on two datasets, one of them being our dataset. However, our dataset does not contain EEG data.**

It all started when I noticed on Google Scholar that the [OSF page](https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/DKTCF) of one of my publications was cited. This page contains the data, code, and materials of a [publication](https://doi.org/10.1111/psyp.14714) on concealed information detection that my co-authors and I published in 2025. The OSF page was cited three times by the same pair of authors: two papers in the Journal of Electronics, Electromedical Engineering, and Medical Informatics (JEEEMI) and one preprint on Elsevier’s preprint server SSRN. It was strange, though, that the papers dealt with EEG-based deception detection, whereas in our study, we collected skin conductance and respiration data, and no EEG data.

At first, I thought it was some kind of reporting error in all three papers. After all, I started reading in good faith – I don’t have experience in machine learning, so perhaps I simply didn’t understand what the authors did. But I didn’t need to get into the machine learning details to find out that our dataset was incorrectly described and that its use was incompatible with the aim of the papers to test machine learning models for EEG-based deception or concealed information detection. For example, the authors reported that we collected data from several EEG channels, that our outcome measures (skin conductance and respiration) were ‘conditions’, and that our participants viewed different types of stimuli, all of which can be marked as factually incorrect. These inaccuracies raise doubts about the accuracy of the findings.

My co-authors and I decided to publicly correct the record about our dataset. We wrote our concerns on the online platform PubPeer ([here](https://pubpeer.com/publications/85A275415249E8202F3A02A3C4CA32), [here](https://pubpeer.com/publications/AA0467B83DECFD1F360B11189D285D), and [here](https://pubpeer.com/publications/B03F4D1E7537C0152DF8E85024CEC0)). In addition, we sent letters including our concerns and links to the PubPeer comments to JEEEMI and SSRN’s support centre. Being aware that a retraction process can take a long time, it was a surprise that the journal editor responded within an hour. He started an investigation and emailed the authors to ask for clarification. The authors responded and within three days the editorial board concluded the investigation and decided to retract both papers. At present, both papers are marked as retracted on the journal’s website, and hopefully they will be formally retracted soon. In the meantime, the preprint is no longer available on SSRN.

Making data openly available is one of the cornerstones of Open Science and is crucial for verifying findings and enabling the reuse of existing data. Yet, open data can also be misused (see also a [recent article on Retraction Watch](https://retractionwatch.com/2026/10/01/openneuro-dataset-fmri-brain-data-misuse/) for another example). Thanks to the very quick and adequate response of the journal editor, our case appears to have been resolved relatively smoothly (pending the formal retractions). Nevertheless, it was a confusing and upsetting experience to see one’s data being reused in an incorrect way, and knowing that it would be difficult to do anything about it. Platforms for post-publication commenting such as PubPeer can be very useful in these cases, as they make it possible to share concerns publicly at an early stage, even when authors or journals do not respond. Fortunately, in our case, they did respond, showing that journals can in fact respond quickly and effectively, and that editorial boards don’t need months or years to clean up the scientific record.
